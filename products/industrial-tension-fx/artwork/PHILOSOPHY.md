# Pressure Cartography

Pressure Cartography treats sound as a physical field that can be mapped only through traces: heat,
density, fracture and absence. The image is not an illustration of machinery. It is evidence left by
an invisible force. Form emerges from accumulated spectral bands, interrupted trajectories and
measured voids, with each mark positioned as if recovered from a precise industrial instrument.

Space behaves like a sealed chamber. Large black regions carry weight rather than emptiness, while a
single concentrated field of colour suggests energy being compressed against a boundary. The balance
must feel controlled but unstable—painstakingly calibrated by someone who understands that tension is
created as much by restraint as by impact.

Colour follows temperature. Near-black graphite is the material base; deep red records sustained
pressure; orange and pale mineral yellow identify the moments where energy becomes visible. No
decorative colour is permitted. Every transition is meticulously tuned so the spectrum feels embedded
in the surface rather than placed on top of it.

Typography acts as sparse instrumentation: one decisive title, one maker's mark, and a small set of
clinical coordinates. Scale creates hierarchy through contrast, not abundance. Letterspacing,
alignment and margins receive the same painstaking attention as the spectral field, giving the object
the authority of a master-level technical plate rather than a marketplace graphic.

The finished work should reward close looking. Its apparent simplicity must reveal countless careful
decisions—microstructure within colour, exact negative space, and a rhythm that echoes pressure rising,
breaking and dissolving. The result is a meticulously crafted artifact: dark, physical and emotionally
contained, with minimal words and no element that cannot justify its presence.
